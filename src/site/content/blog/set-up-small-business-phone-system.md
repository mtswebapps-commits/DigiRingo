Setting up a business phone system used to mean an installer, a closet full of equipment and a contract. For most small businesses today, it means an app, a few decisions and an afternoon. The technology is the easy part. The planning is what makes the system work well.

This guide is a checklist you can follow from start to finish. It covers who should answer, which numbers to get, what happens when nobody picks up, how to get texting approved, and how to test everything before customers start calling.

Grab a notepad, block out three or four hours, and work through it in order.

> **Key takeaway:** Plan first, then build. Decide who answers which calls and what happens when nobody does, before you buy a single number. The setup itself takes minutes; the decisions take the afternoon.

## Step 1: Plan your lines and who answers

Before you open any app, answer these questions on paper:

- **How many numbers do you need?** Many businesses start with one main number. Some add a second for sales, support or a different location.
- **Who answers each number?** Write down names, and the backup if they're busy.
- **When are you open?** Write your hours for each day.
- **What happens when nobody answers?** Forward to a mobile? Voicemail? Both?
- **Will you text customers?** If yes, what kinds of messages: reminders, quotes, support?
- **Do you call internationally,** or need numbers in other countries?

A simple plan for a three-person business might look like this:

| Number | Purpose | Rings | If unanswered |
|---|---|---|---|
| Main local number | All customer calls and texts | Owner's laptop and phone | Forward to office manager's mobile, then voicemail |
| Second local number | Outbound sales calls | Sales rep's phone | Voicemail |

Keep it simple. You can always add complexity later.

## Step 2: Choose and claim your numbers

Now decide what kind of numbers fit your customers:

- **Local numbers** feel familiar to people in your area and are the usual choice for service businesses.
- **Toll-free numbers** suit businesses with customers nationwide. Our guide to [local vs toll-free numbers](/blog/local-vs-toll-free-number) covers the trade-offs.
- **International numbers** let customers in other countries call you locally. Some countries require ID or address documents before the number is activated.

Tips for picking:

- Choose an area code that matches where your customers are.
- Look for a number that's easy to say and remember.
- If you already have a number customers know, you'll want to keep it. Moving an existing number between providers is called porting, explained in [what number porting is](/blog/what-is-number-porting).

## Step 3: Decide your hours and greeting

Write down your business hours and how you'll handle calls outside them. Then write a short voicemail script, even if you won't record it today.

A good greeting is short: "You've reached Harbor Electric. We can't take your call right now. Please leave your name, number and a short message, and we'll call you back by the next business morning."

Depending on your phone system, you might be able to set hours that route calls automatically and record a custom greeting. If yours can't do that yet, you can still publish your hours on your website and listings, and follow a consistent routine for returning after-hours calls the next morning.

## Step 4: Configure forwarding and voicemail

This is where you decide what happens to calls nobody picks up. A good fallback chain looks like this:

1. **Ring the app** on every device you're signed in to.
2. **Forward to a mobile** if nobody answers after about 20 to 30 seconds.
3. **Send to voicemail** as the last stop.

Things to check:

- **Ring time.** Too short and you miss calls; too long and callers hang up. Around 20 to 30 seconds per step is a common choice.
- **Mobile voicemail.** If you forward to a mobile, its own voicemail may grab the call before your business voicemail does. Our post on [conditional call forwarding](/blog/conditional-call-forwarding) explains how to avoid that.
- **Caller ID on outbound calls.** Make sure you call customers from your business number, not your personal one.

## Step 5: Register for business texting

In the US, business texts from regular local numbers need A2P 10DLC registration. Unregistered business texts may be filtered or blocked by carriers. Registration has two parts:

- **Brand:** your business's legal name, tax ID, address and contact details.
- **Campaign:** what kind of messages you'll send, sample messages, and how customers agree to receive them.

Approval usually takes from a day to about a week, so start this early. While you wait, you can make and receive calls normally.

Have this ready before you start:

| Information | Example |
|---|---|
| Legal business name | Harbor Electric LLC |
| Tax ID (EIN in the US) | From your registration documents |
| Business address and website | As listed publicly |
| Message use case | Appointment reminders and customer support |
| Sample messages | "Hi Lisa, this is Harbor Electric confirming your visit Thursday at 10am. Reply STOP to opt out." |
| How customers opt in | Checkbox on booking form, or customer texts you first |

## Step 6: Set up your team and test everything

If more than one person will answer, get everyone signed in on their devices and walk through the plan from Step 1 together.

Then test. This is the step people skip, and it's the one that catches problems:

1. **Call your number from a mobile** and check every device rings.
2. **Let it ring out** and confirm forwarding kicks in.
3. **Let the forwarded call ring out** and confirm it reaches voicemail.
4. **Leave a voicemail** and make sure you can find it.
5. **Make an outbound call** and check the caller ID shows your business number.
6. **Send and receive a text** once registration is approved.
7. **Test from a different network,** like mobile data instead of office Wi-Fi.

Finally, update your website, Google Business Profile, email signatures and any printed materials with the new number.

## Frequently asked questions

### How long does it take to set up a small business phone system?

The setup itself can take under an hour with an app-based system. Planning, testing and texting registration make it closer to an afternoon, and texting approval can take a few more days.

### Do I need a desk phone?

No. Most small businesses use a computer and their mobile. If you prefer a desk phone, look for a system that supports SIP devices.

### How much does a small business phone system cost?

With pay-as-you-go services, a single local number plus moderate calling can cost just a few dollars a month. Costs rise with more numbers, more minutes and international calls.

### Can I keep my existing business number?

Usually yes, through porting. Check whether your new provider supports porting; DIGIRINGO doesn't offer it yet, though it's coming soon.

### Do I have to register to send business texts?

In the US, texting from local numbers for business purposes generally requires A2P 10DLC registration. Without it, carriers may filter or block your messages.
