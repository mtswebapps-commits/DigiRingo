Your business number is probably printed on your website, your invoices, your Google profile and a few hundred customers' contact lists. That's why the fear of losing it keeps many businesses stuck with a phone provider they no longer like.

The good news is that in the US and Canada, and in many other countries, you have the right to take your number with you. The process is called **number porting**. It's routine, regulated and done millions of times a year. It just involves a bit of paperwork and some patience.

This guide walks through the whole lifecycle of a port in plain steps: what it is, which numbers qualify, what documents you need, what happens to your calls during the switch, and how to check everything works afterward.

> **Key takeaway:** You can almost always keep your number when you switch providers. Gather your account details first, don't cancel your old service until the port is complete, and test calls and texts the day the number moves.

## What porting is and who controls it

Porting means transferring a phone number from one carrier or provider (the "losing" carrier) to another (the "gaining" carrier). The number stays exactly the same. Only the company that handles calls to it changes.

In the US, the right to port comes from federal rules known as Local Number Portability, overseen by the FCC. Canada has similar rules overseen by the CRTC. Under these rules, your current carrier can't refuse to release a number just because they'd rather keep you as a customer, as long as your request is valid.

A few key points about who controls what:

- **The account holder controls the number.** Only the person or business named on the account can authorize a port.
- **The gaining provider submits the request.** You don't call your old carrier to "send" the number. Your new provider asks for it on your behalf.
- **The losing carrier validates it.** They check your details match their records, then agree to a transfer date.
- **The losing carrier can't charge you to leave**, although you're still responsible for any contract terms, early termination fees or unpaid balances on the old account.

## Which numbers can be ported

Most numbers can be ported, but not every number can go to every provider.

| Number type | Usually portable? | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile numbers | Yes | Often the fastest type to port |
| Landline numbers | Yes | Business lines may take longer |
| VoIP numbers | Usually | Depends on the gaining provider's coverage |
| Toll-free numbers | Yes | Uses a separate toll-free process |
| International numbers | Varies | Rules differ by country |
| Free app numbers | Sometimes | Some free services restrict or charge to port out |

The main limit is coverage. A provider can only accept a local number if it operates in that number's rate centre. Before you start, ask your new provider to check that your number is "portable" to them. Most can run this check instantly.

## Documents you'll need

Gathering this before you submit saves days. Get it straight from your current carrier's latest bill or account portal:

- **Account holder name** exactly as the carrier has it, including the business name if it's a business account.
- **Service address** on file with the carrier. This is sometimes different from your billing address.
- **Account number.** For mobile accounts this may differ from the number on your bill, so check.
- **Port-out PIN or passcode.** Many mobile carriers now require a special transfer PIN that you generate in their app or by calling them.
- **A recent bill**, which some providers ask you to upload as proof.
- **A signed Letter of Authorization (LOA)**, which your new provider usually generates for you to sign electronically.

The most common reason ports fail is that these details don't match exactly. We cover fixes for each rejection reason in [why number ports get rejected](/blog/number-port-rejected-fixes).

## The step-by-step process

Here's how a typical port unfolds:

1. **Open your new account** and, if offered, get a temporary number so you can start using the service right away.
2. **Check portability** of the number you want to bring.
3. **Submit the port request** with your account details and signed LOA.
4. **The losing carrier reviews it.** They check the name, address, account number and PIN.
5. **You get a confirmed date.** In the industry this is called the FOC (Firm Order Commitment) date.
6. **The number moves on the FOC date.** Calls begin routing to the new provider, usually within a short window that day.
7. **You test and confirm** everything works.
8. **Then you cancel the old service**, if it wasn't cancelled automatically.

How long this takes depends on the number type and how clean your paperwork is. Simple mobile ports can complete within a business day, while business landlines and multi-number accounts can take a week or more. See [how long porting takes](/blog/how-long-does-porting-take) for realistic timelines.

## What happens to calls during the port

Before the FOC date, nothing changes. Your number keeps working on your old service as normal. On the day itself, there can be a short window where some calls reach the old provider and some reach the new one, as carriers across the network update their routing.

To keep that window painless:

- **Don't cancel your old service early.** Cancelling before the port completes can release the number, and once a number is disconnected it may be hard or impossible to get back.
- **Pick a quiet day** if you can choose. Avoid your busiest day of the week.
- **Set up the new service fully in advance**, so the number works the moment it lands: routing, forwarding and voicemail.
- **Watch both services on port day**, answering anything that still arrives on the old line.

Texting is often the last thing to move. SMS routing is handled separately from voice, so text messages can take a little longer to start arriving at the new provider. In the US, if you text customers from the number, you'll also need it registered for 10DLC with your new provider. Plan for that registration to take a few days.

## After the port: testing checklist

Once your new provider confirms the port is complete, run through these checks:

| Test | How | What you're checking |
|---|---|---|
| Inbound from mobile | Call the number from a cellphone | Calls reach the new service |
| Inbound from landline | Call from a different carrier if possible | All networks have updated |
| Outbound caller ID | Call out and check what the recipient sees | The ported number displays |
| Voicemail | Let a call go unanswered | Voicemail picks up on the new service |
| Forwarding | Test any forwarding rules | Calls reach your backup phone |
| SMS in and out | Send a text both ways | Messaging has moved over |

If calls from one particular carrier still go to the old provider a day later, tell your new provider. A routing record somewhere may need updating.

## Frequently asked questions

### Will I lose my number if I switch providers?

No, as long as you port it before cancelling the old service. Cancelling first is the main way people lose a number.

### Can my old carrier refuse to port my number?

They can reject a request if the details don't match their records, but they can't block a valid port. You still owe any contract fees or balances.

### Do I need to tell my old carrier I'm leaving?

Generally no. Your new provider submits the request, and the old account usually closes for that number once the port completes. Confirm with your old carrier afterward.

### Does porting move my texts and voicemails?

No. Only the number moves. Save any old voicemails or message history you want before the port completes.
