Plenty of people who want a separate business line assume they need a second phone, a second SIM card, or a desk phone bolted to the wall. In most cases they don't. A virtual phone number gives you a real, dialable phone number that lives in the cloud instead of on a piece of hardware, and it can ring the phone and computer you already own.

The idea sounds technical, but it isn't. If you have used WhatsApp calling or a video call in your browser, you have already used the same technology. A virtual number just puts a normal phone number in front of it, so anyone with any phone can reach you.

This guide explains how a number can exist without a SIM card, what happens when somebody dials it, how it compares to mobile and landline numbers, and how to decide whether you need one.

> **Key takeaway:** A virtual phone number is a regular phone number that is hosted by a provider in the cloud rather than tied to a SIM card or copper line. You answer it in an app or browser, and you can forward it to your existing cellphone, so you get a second line without a second device.

## How a number can exist without a SIM card

A phone number is really just an address on the global phone network. For a long time that address was physically tied to something: a copper pair running into your building for a landline, or a SIM card for a mobile phone. The phone company used that physical link to know where to send your calls.

Modern phone networks don't need that physical link anymore. Carriers can route a number to an internet address instead of a copper wire or a SIM. When a provider like DIGIRINGO gives you a virtual number, the carrier records "calls to this number go to this provider," and the provider decides where to deliver them: your browser, your phone app, or another phone number.

That's why a virtual number is sometimes called a cloud number or a VoIP number. VoIP stands for Voice over Internet Protocol, which simply means your voice travels as data over the internet for at least part of the trip.
## What happens behind the scenes when someone calls it

Here is a simplified walk-through of one incoming call:

1. A customer dials your number from their mobile phone, the same way they would dial anyone else.
2. Their carrier looks up who is responsible for that number and hands the call to your provider's network.
3. Your provider checks how you have set up the number. It might ring your apps, forward to a cellphone, or go to voicemail.
4. If it rings your apps, every device you are signed in on starts ringing at the same moment.
5. You tap answer on whichever device is closest, and the conversation flows as internet data between you and the provider, then over the normal phone network to the caller.

The caller has no idea any of this happened. To them it is an ordinary phone call to an ordinary number. Outgoing calls work the same way in reverse: you tap a contact in the app, the provider places the call on the phone network, and the other person sees your virtual number as the caller ID.

## Virtual vs mobile vs landline numbers

All three types of number can look identical. The difference is what sits behind them.

| | Virtual number | Mobile number | Landline number |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tied to hardware | No | Yes, a SIM or eSIM | Yes, a physical line |
| Rings on | App, browser, or forwarded phone | One handset | Phones wired to that line |
| Works abroad | Yes, wherever you have internet | Usually with roaming charges | No |
| Setup time | Minutes | Store visit or eSIM activation | Days to weeks |
| Second line on the same phone | Yes | Only with dual SIM | No |
| Typical cost model | Monthly number fee plus usage | Monthly plan | Monthly line rental plus calls |

The big practical difference is flexibility. A mobile number is stuck in one handset. A landline is stuck in one building. A virtual number goes wherever you are signed in, and it doesn't care whether that's a laptop in your office or an Android phone on a job site.

## Things you can do with a virtual number

What you can do depends on the provider and the country of the number, but a good virtual number service covers the basics a business needs:

- **Make and receive calls** from a web browser or a mobile app, with your business number as caller ID.
- **Send and receive text messages**, kept in threaded conversations separate from your personal texts.
- **Forward calls** to another phone, so if you don't pick up in the app the call reaches your cellphone.
- **Get voicemail** when nobody answers.
- **Keep call history** for your business line in one place.
- **Hold several numbers** at once, for example a local number in your city and a toll-free number for a national audience.

One note on texting in the US: carriers require business texting from regular local numbers to be registered under a system called A2P 10DLC before messages are delivered reliably. That's a carrier rule, not a provider quirk, and any legitimate provider will walk you through it. Our explainer on [what A2P 10DLC is](/blog/what-is-a2p-10dlc) covers it in plain language.

## Who actually needs a virtual number

You don't need a virtual number just because they exist. They solve specific problems well:

- **Solo owners and freelancers** who currently hand out their personal cell number and want to stop. A second number keeps work and life apart without carrying two phones.
- **Small teams** who want their business line to ring on laptops as well as phones.
- **Remote and traveling workers** who need to call customers from anywhere with Wi-Fi without paying roaming rates.
- **Businesses selling into another region or country**, who want a local number there so customers don't have to place an international call.
- **Side businesses and new ventures** that want a number for a website, ads, or marketplace listings they can stop paying for later.

If you only ever take personal calls and never give your number to customers, you probably don't need one. If your personal phone number is printed on your van, your invoices, and your Google listing, you almost certainly do. ## Common misconceptions

**"Virtual numbers are fake numbers."** They are real numbers assigned through the same carrier system as any other. People can call and text them from any phone.

**"You need special equipment."** You need an internet connection and a device you already own: a laptop, a desktop, or a smartphone. Desk phones are optional.

**"Call quality is worse."** On a decent connection, internet calls usually sound as good as or better than a mobile call. Quality problems almost always come from the network you are on, and they are fixable.

**"Every app and bank will accept it for verification codes."** Not always. Some services refuse to send one-time passcodes to internet-based numbers. That's a decision made by the service sending the code, so don't rely on a virtual number for account security codes.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is a virtual phone number a real number?

Yes. It is a normal phone number issued through the carrier system. Anyone can call or text it from a landline or mobile, and they won't see anything unusual.

### Do I need a second phone to use a virtual number?

No. You answer it in an app on your current phone or in a web browser on your computer, and you can forward unanswered calls to your existing cellphone.

### Can I text from a virtual number?

Usually yes, depending on the number and country. In the US and Canada, business texting from local numbers requires A2P 10DLC registration, which DIGIRINGO guides you through in its Trust Center.

### Can I use a virtual number while traveling?

Yes. As long as you have an internet connection, you can make and take calls on your business number from anywhere, without roaming charges for those calls.

### Will a virtual number work for bank or app verification codes?

Sometimes, but not reliably. Some services block internet-based numbers from receiving one-time passcodes, so keep your personal mobile number on file for security codes.

### How long does it take to get a virtual number?

For US and Canadian numbers, usually a few minutes. Numbers in some other countries need documents reviewed by the carrier first, which typically takes 1 to 3 days.
