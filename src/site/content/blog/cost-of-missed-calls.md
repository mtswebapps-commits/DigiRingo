A missed call doesn't show up as a line on your bank statement. There's no invoice for the customer who rang twice, got no answer and booked someone else. That's exactly why the cost is easy to ignore: it's invisible.

You can make it visible with a bit of arithmetic. You don't need industry reports or someone else's averages. Your own call history and a few honest guesses about your business are enough to estimate what missed calls are worth to you.

This guide walks through a worked example with every assumption laid out, so you can swap in your own numbers. Then it ranks the fixes from easiest to most involved.

> **Key takeaway:** The value of a missed call is roughly: share of missed callers who were potential new business × share who would have bought × average sale value. Multiply by your missed calls per month, and you have a number worth acting on.

## How to find out how many calls you miss

You'll see plenty of headline figures online about how many calls small businesses miss. Ignore them. Every business is different, and your own number is the only one that matters.

Here's how to get it:

1. **Open your call history** for the last four weeks. Most business phone apps and many carriers show answered and missed calls.
2. **Count total incoming calls** and **missed incoming calls**.
3. **Remove obvious noise:** spam, robocalls, and repeat attempts from the same number within a few minutes (count those as one missed call).
4. **Divide missed by total** to get your miss rate.

If you don't have a call log, keep a tally on paper for two weeks. It's tedious, but it's better than guessing.

## Calculating what a missed call costs you

Here's a worked example for a fictional home-services business. These are assumptions, not facts about any real company. Replace each one with your own.

| Input | Example value | Where to get your own |
|---|---|---|
| Incoming calls per week | 40 | Your call history |
| Miss rate | 20% | Missed calls ÷ total calls |
| Missed calls per week | 8 | Calls × miss rate |
| Share of missed callers who are potential new customers | 50% | Look back at who called; how many were new numbers? |
| Share of those who would have booked if answered | 40% | Your booking rate on answered calls from new customers |
| Average first job value | $250 | Your invoices |

Now the arithmetic:

1. Missed calls per week: 40 × 20% = **8**
2. Potential new customers among them: 8 × 50% = **4**
3. Bookings you'd likely have won: 4 × 40% = **1.6**
4. Revenue lost per week: 1.6 × $250 = **$400**
5. Revenue lost per year (50 working weeks): $400 × 50 = **$20,000**
6. Average value of each missed call: $400 ÷ 8 = **$50**

That last number is useful. In this example, every time the phone rings out, it costs about $50 on average, even though most missed calls were never going to turn into a sale.

## How sensitive the result is to your assumptions

Your numbers will be different, and some inputs move the result a lot. Here's the same example with only the miss rate and job value changed, keeping the other assumptions (50% new, 40% would book, 40 calls a week):

| Miss rate | $100 job | $250 job | $1,000 job |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5% | $40/week | $100/week | $400/week |
| 10% | $80/week | $200/week | $800/week |
| 20% | $160/week | $400/week | $1,600/week |

Each cell is 40 calls × miss rate × 50% × 40% × job value. For example, 40 × 20% × 50% × 40% = 1.6 bookings, and 1.6 × $250 = $400. Run the same formula in a spreadsheet with your own inputs. The point of the exercise isn't precision. It's to see whether the cost is small enough to ignore or large enough to fix.

Two more things to consider:

- **Repeat business.** If a typical customer comes back several times a year, the true cost of losing them is much higher than one job.
- **Referrals.** A customer who never reached you also never recommends you.

## Why callers don't leave voicemail

You might think missed calls are fine because callers can leave a message. In practice, many don't, especially new customers. A few common reasons:

- **They have other options.** A new customer searching online has a list of businesses and simply calls the next one.
- **Voicemail feels slow.** They don't know when, or if, you'll call back.
- **They're calling about something urgent** and need an answer now.
- **They dislike leaving messages** in general.

That's why fixes that get a live response, or at least a quick personal follow-up, tend to work better than relying on voicemail alone.

## Quick fixes, ranked by effort

Start at the top and work down. Each one closes some of the gap.

1. **Call back fast.** Check missed calls every hour or two and call back. It costs nothing and often saves the lead.
2. **Ring more than one device.** If calls only ring a desk phone, you miss everything when you step away. Ringing your laptop and your mobile at the same time catches far more.
3. **Forward unanswered calls to a cellphone.** If the app isn't answered within a set time, send the call to your mobile or a teammate's. See [conditional call forwarding](/blog/conditional-call-forwarding) for the options.
4. **Text missed callers.** A short text like "Sorry we missed your call, how can we help?" turns a missed call into a conversation. Our guide to [missed-call text back](/blog/missed-call-text-back) has wording you can use.
5. **Set up voicemail** with a clear greeting, so those who do want to leave a message can.
6. **Add coverage at peak times.** If your call log shows most missed calls happen at the same time of day, that's when you need another person on the phone.

## Covering after-hours calls

Some missed calls happen simply because you're closed. Decide what you want for those callers:

- **Voicemail with a callback promise,** returned first thing in the morning.
- **Forwarding to an on-call phone** for urgent work, if your business offers emergency service.
- **A morning callback routine,** working through missed calls oldest first.

Whatever you choose, be consistent. A caller who learns you always call back by 9am is much more forgiving than one who never hears from you.

## Measuring improvement

Once you've made changes, run the same calculation again after four weeks:

- **Miss rate:** has it dropped?
- **Callback speed:** how long between a missed call and your return call?
- **Bookings from new callers:** are you winning more?

Keep a simple monthly record. Even a rough before-and-after comparison tells you whether a change was worth it.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I calculate the cost of a missed call?

Multiply your missed calls by the share that were potential new customers, then by your booking rate, then by your average sale. Divide the result by missed calls to get the average cost per missed call.

### What is a normal missed call rate for a small business?

It varies widely by industry and staffing, so the most useful benchmark is your own past performance. Measure it for four weeks, make changes, and measure again.

### Is a callback as good as answering the first time?

Not quite, but a fast callback recovers many leads, especially within the first hour. The longer you wait, the more likely the caller has found someone else.

### Do missed calls from existing customers matter?

Yes. They may not cost a sale today, but repeated unanswered calls damage trust and can push loyal customers to look elsewhere.
