The first decision when you set up a business phone line is what kind of number to get. In the US and Canada, the choice usually comes down to two options: a local number with a city area code, or a toll-free number starting with 800, 888 or one of their siblings.

Both work the same way from a technical point of view. Calls come in, you answer them, you call people back. The differences are in how customers perceive the number, who pays for the call, and the rules that apply when you want to send text messages.

This guide walks through those differences and gives you a simple way to decide, including when it makes sense to have both.

> **Key takeaway:** Choose a local number if your customers are mostly in one area and you want to feel close and personal, and to text through A2P 10DLC. Choose toll-free if you serve customers nationally and want a single number that feels established. Many businesses use a local number for daily contact and a toll-free number for national marketing.

## What each number type signals to callers

Numbers carry meaning before anyone says hello.

**A local number** tells callers you're nearby. A plumber, dentist or real estate agent with a familiar area code feels like part of the community. People are also more likely to pick up a call from a local area code than an unknown toll-free number, because many associate toll-free numbers with call centers and sales calls.

**A toll-free number** signals size and reach. It suggests a business that serves the whole country and has a proper customer line. For national brands, online stores, and support lines, that's often exactly the message you want. Toll-free numbers also hide where you're located, which can help a small business look bigger or avoid being seen as "out of town."

| | Local number | Toll-free number |
|---|---|---|
| Looks like | (312) 555-0142 | (800) 555-0142 |
| Signals | Nearby, personal, community | National, established, customer service |
| Who pays for the call | Caller pays usual rates (often included in their plan) | The business pays for incoming calls |
| Answer rate on outgoing calls | Often higher, especially locally | Often lower, can look like sales calls |
| Texting registration | A2P 10DLC | Toll-free verification |
| Best for | Local service businesses, sales outreach | National brands, support lines, ads |

## Cost differences

For callers, toll-free numbers are free to dial, though today most mobile plans include nationwide calling anyway, so "free to call" matters less than it once did.

For your business, the cost picture flips. With a toll-free number, the business pays for incoming minutes because the caller isn't charged. Some providers charge more per month for a toll-free number or a higher rate on incoming toll-free minutes. With a local number, incoming calls are typically priced at the standard rate.

Check three things when comparing:

1. The monthly price for each number type.
2. The per-minute rate for incoming calls on each type.
3. The per-minute rate for outgoing calls, which is usually the same for both.
## Texting rules: 10DLC vs toll-free verification

If you plan to text customers, this section matters more than any other.

In the US, carriers treat business texting from different number types under different registration systems:

- **Local numbers** use **A2P 10DLC**. You register your business as a brand and describe what kind of messages you'll send as a campaign. Approval generally takes days, and carriers charge registration fees. Once approved, local numbers are a good fit for conversational texting, reminders and customer updates. Our guide to [A2P 10DLC](/blog/what-is-a2p-10dlc) explains the process in detail.
- **Toll-free numbers** use **toll-free verification**. You submit details about your business, your use case, how customers opt in, and sample messages. Unverified toll-free numbers face heavy filtering or blocking for business texts.

Both systems exist for the same reason: to cut down on spam and make sure carriers know who's sending messages and why. Neither is a shortcut. Which one is easier depends on your use case and your provider's support.

One practical point: some businesses are tempted to pick a toll-free number for texting only to discover their provider doesn't support toll-free texting. Always confirm what each number type can do before you print it on anything.

## When local wins

A local number is usually the better choice if:

- Most of your customers live in one city or region.
- You make outbound calls and want people to pick up.
- You want a friendly, personal brand.
- You plan to text customers regularly, and your provider supports 10DLC registration.
- You're opening in a new city and want to look established there. You can get a number with that city's area code even if you're based elsewhere.

Local service businesses such as trades, salons, clinics, restaurants and real estate agents tend to do well with local numbers. If you serve several towns, pick the area code your best customers recognize most.

## When toll-free wins

A toll-free number tends to win if:

- You sell across the whole country and don't want to look tied to one city.
- You run national advertising and want one consistent number.
- You have a customer support line that people call more than you call them.
- You want a vanity number that spells a word, which is often easier to find in toll-free inventory.
- Your brand benefits from looking larger than it is.

Toll-free numbers in North America use the prefixes 800, 888, 877, 866, 855, 844 and 833. Callers treat all of them the same way, though 800 numbers are the most recognizable and the most heavily used.

## Why many businesses use both

You don't have to pick only one. A common setup:

- A **toll-free number** on the website and in national ads, for inbound calls.
- One or more **local numbers** for outbound calls, texting customers, and local listings in each city you serve.

Since both numbers can ring the same app and devices, there's no extra work to answer them. You simply choose which number to show when you call out.

If you're expanding into Canada, the same logic applies. Toll-free numbers work across the US and Canada, while local Canadian numbers help you look local in a specific province. Our guide to [getting a Canadian business number](/blog/get-canadian-business-number) explains the details.

It depends on the context. Callers often trust toll-free numbers for national brands and support lines, but they're more likely to answer an outbound call from a local area code.
