You call a customer who's expecting to hear from you, and they don't pick up. Later they tell you their phone showed "Spam Likely" or "Scam Risk" under your number. For a legitimate business, that's frustrating: you're not doing anything wrong, but your calls are being treated like robocalls.

This happens more often than most owners realise. Carriers and call-blocking apps use automated systems to protect their customers from fraud, and those systems sometimes catch honest businesses too. The good news is that a flagged number can usually be cleared, and most flags can be prevented with better calling habits.

This guide explains how spam labels get applied, what the STIR/SHAKEN framework does, and the practical steps to fix and prevent the problem.

> **Key takeaway:** Spam labels come from carrier analytics that look at how a number behaves, not who owns it. Register your number with the analytics providers, call at a sensible pace, keep calls short of robocall patterns, and give customers a reason to expect you.

## How carriers decide a call is spam

When you place a call, the receiving carrier, and often a third-party analytics company working with it, scores the call in real time. If the score crosses a threshold, the recipient's phone shows a warning label, sends the call to voicemail, or blocks it.

The exact formulas are private, but the signals they look at are broadly known:

- **Volume.** Many calls from one number in a short time.
- **Short call duration.** Lots of calls that last only a few seconds.
- **Low answer rate.** Most people don't pick up.
- **Call patterns.** Dialling numbers in sequence, or calling many numbers the caller has never contacted before.
- **Complaints.** People reporting the number as spam, or blocking it.
- **Number age and history.** A brand-new number, or one with a bad past under a previous owner.
- **Authentication.** Whether the call carries a verified caller ID signature.

No single signal is decisive. A busy sales line that gets reported a few times can be flagged even if every call is legitimate.

## STIR/SHAKEN in plain words

STIR/SHAKEN is a framework US and Canadian carriers use to fight spoofed caller ID, where scammers fake the number that shows on your screen.

Here's the short version:

1. When a call starts, the originating provider digitally signs it, saying how confident it is that the caller is allowed to use that number.
2. The signature travels with the call across networks.
3. The receiving carrier checks the signature. A valid, high-confidence signature means the caller ID is very likely genuine.

There are three confidence levels, often called attestation levels:

| Level | What it means |
|---|---|
| A (full) | The provider knows the customer and confirms they're authorised to use the number |
| B (partial) | The provider knows the customer but can't confirm the number is theirs |
| C (gateway) | The provider is passing the call along and can't vouch for its origin |

An important point: STIR/SHAKEN proves *who* is calling. It doesn't prove the call is wanted. A fully authenticated number can still be labelled spam if its calling behaviour looks like a robocaller's.

## Calling habits that get you flagged

Most legitimate businesses get flagged because of how they call, not what they say. Watch out for these patterns:

- **Bursts of outbound calls,** such as calling through a list of 200 leads in an hour from one number.
- **Many very short calls,** for example hanging up as soon as voicemail answers.
- **Calling people who don't know you,** especially cold lists bought from third parties.
- **Repeated calls to the same person** in a short time.
- **Using one number for everything,** so a high-volume outreach campaign drags down the number your existing customers rely on.
- **Calling at odd hours,** which leads to more complaints and blocks.

## How to check if your number is flagged

There's no single public lookup, but you can get a good picture:

1. **Call your own mobile** from your business number, ideally on phones from several different carriers (ask friends or colleagues). Note what each screen shows.
2. **Install a popular call-screening app** on a test phone and see how it labels your number.
3. **Search your number online.** Community sites where people report unwanted callers sometimes list business numbers.
4. **Watch your answer rate.** A sudden drop in pick-ups often means a label appeared.
5. **Ask customers.** If someone mentions a warning, ask them which carrier they use and what it said.

Write down each carrier and label you see. You'll need it when requesting a fix.

## Getting a number unflagged

Once you know where the label is coming from, here's the usual process:

1. **Register your number with the analytics providers.** The major call-analytics companies behind many US carrier labels accept business registrations, and some run a shared registration portal so you can submit once. Search for "free caller registry" to find it. You'll typically provide your business name, contact details, the numbers you call from and why you call.
2. **Use the carrier's or app's dispute form.** Most major carriers and call-blocking apps have a page where you can report a number that's been mislabelled. Look for "report incorrect spam label" or similar on their websites.
3. **Ask your phone provider for help.** They may be able to confirm your calls are signed correctly and suggest next steps.
4. **Fix the behaviour first.** If the label was caused by high-volume dialling, slow down before or while you dispute it, or it'll likely come back.
5. **Give it time.** Reviews often take days to a few weeks. Check again with test calls after each step.

If a number keeps getting flagged even after disputes, it can be quicker to move outbound calling to a fresh number and keep your main number for inbound and existing customers.

## Preventing it from happening again

| Habit | Why it helps |
|---|---|
| Separate numbers for outreach and for customer service | Protects your main number's reputation |
| Pace outbound calls | Avoids volume spikes that look automated |
| Let calls ring at least 20 to 30 seconds | Reduces the very short calls that look like robocalls |
| Leave a voicemail with your name and business | Makes the call recognisable and reduces reports |
| Text first when appropriate | Customers who expect your call are more likely to answer and less likely to report |
| Only call people with a reason to hear from you | Fewer complaints and blocks |
| Register new numbers before you start calling | Analytics providers know who you are from day one |

Texting first deserves a note: in the US, business texts should come from a number registered for A2P 10DLC and go only to people who agreed to hear from you. See our [A2P 10DLC guide](/blog/what-is-a2p-10dlc) for the basics.

## Frequently asked questions

### Why does my new business number show as spam?

New numbers sometimes inherit a bad reputation from a previous owner, or get flagged quickly because there's no calling history yet. Registering the number with the analytics providers before you make many calls helps.

### Can I pay to remove a spam label?

The main registration and dispute routes are generally free. Be cautious of services that promise guaranteed removal for a fee; check exactly what they do before paying.

### Does STIR/SHAKEN stop my calls from being labelled spam?

Not by itself. It confirms your caller ID is genuine, which helps, but carriers still label numbers based on calling behaviour and complaints.

### How long does it take to clear a spam label?

It depends on the carrier and analytics provider. Some changes show up within days; others take a few weeks. Keep testing with calls to phones on different carriers.

### Should I switch numbers if mine is flagged?

Try registering and disputing first, especially if customers know your number. If the label keeps coming back, move outbound calling to a new number and keep the old one for incoming calls.
