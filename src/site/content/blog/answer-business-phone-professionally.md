Most small businesses don't have a receptionist. The person who answers the phone might be the owner, a technician between jobs, or whoever is closest to the laptop. That's normal. But callers still form an opinion in the first few seconds, and a rushed "Hello?" from an unknown voice doesn't inspire much confidence.

The good news is that sounding professional on the phone is a learnable skill, not a personality trait. It comes down to a few habits and some words you can rehearse until they're automatic.

This guide gives you scripts for each part of a call: the greeting, putting someone on hold, passing them to someone else, taking a message, handling an upset caller, and ending the call. Adapt the wording so it sounds like you.

> **Key takeaway:** A professional call has a predictable shape: greet with your business name and your own name, find out what the caller needs, tell them what happens next, and confirm before you hang up. Scripts help you hit each step even on a busy day.

## The first five seconds

Callers decide fast whether they've reached the right place. In the first five seconds, they want to know three things: Is this the business I meant to call? Is a real person listening? Can this person help me?

A few habits make those seconds count:

- **Answer within three or four rings.** Picking up on the first ring can catch you mid-sentence; waiting too long makes people hang up.
- **Pause before you speak.** Take a breath and finish what you were doing. A half-second of quiet is better than a distracted greeting.
- **Smile.** It sounds like a cliché, but it changes your tone in a way callers can hear.
- **Lower background noise.** Step away from the radio, the drill or the coffee machine if you can.
- **Speak a little slower than normal.** Your business name is familiar to you and brand new to the caller.

## Greeting scripts that work

A good greeting has your business name, your name, and an offer to help. Keep it under eight seconds.

1. "Good morning, Harbor Electric, this is Sam. How can I help?"
2. "Thanks for calling Willow Pet Care, you're speaking with Priya."
3. "Northside Bakery, this is Tom. What can I do for you today?"
4. "Hi, Delgado Law Office, Maria speaking."
5. "Thanks for calling Peak Cleaning. This is Jordan, how can I help?"

For a solo business answering on a mobile device, the same idea applies: "Hi, this is Alex with Alex Martin Design." It tells the caller they reached a business, not your personal line.

Avoid greetings that are too long ("Thank you for calling Harbor Electric, your trusted partner for residential and commercial electrical solutions since 2009...") or too vague ("Hello?").

## Finding out what the caller needs

After the greeting, your job is to understand the request quickly. Use open questions first, then confirm with a closed one.

- "What can I help you with today?"
- "Can you tell me a bit more about what's happening?"
- "Just to make sure I've got it, you'd like to book a cleaning for next Thursday?"

Write down the caller's name early and use it once or twice. It shows you're listening and helps if you need to take a message later.

## Putting callers on hold

Hold is fine when it's short and explained. The problem is unannounced silence.

| Situation | What to say |
|---|---|
| Quick check | "Can I put you on a short hold while I check the schedule? It'll be about a minute." |
| Longer task | "This might take three or four minutes. Would you like to hold, or should I call you back?" |
| Returning from hold | "Thanks for waiting, Lisa. I've got the answer." |
| Still checking | "I'm still looking into it. Can you hold another minute, or shall I call you back?" |

Rules of thumb:

- **Always ask before holding.** "Can I put you on hold?" not "Hold please."
- **Give a time estimate,** and be honest.
- **Check back every 60 to 90 seconds** if the hold runs long.
- **Offer a callback** for anything over a couple of minutes.

## Passing a caller to someone else

When the caller needs someone else, the worst thing you can do is send them into the unknown. Explain who they'll speak to and why.

- "The best person for this is Dana, who handles billing. Let me check if she's free."
- "Dana's on another call. Can I take your number and have her call you back within the hour?"

If your phone system can transfer calls, introduce the caller to your colleague before connecting them, so the caller doesn't have to repeat everything. If it can't, a promised callback with a specific time works just as well, and often better, because the right person calls back prepared.

## Taking a message properly

A message is only useful if the person receiving it can act on it without calling you first. Capture these every time:

1. Caller's full name, with spelling if it's unusual
2. Best callback number, read back to confirm
3. What the call is about, in one or two sentences
4. How urgent it is and the best time to call back
5. The date and time of the call
6. Your name, so the recipient knows who took it

Script: "I'll make sure Dana gets this. Can I get your name and the best number to reach you? ... Thanks. And briefly, what's it about? ... Great. She's usually back by 3, so expect a call this afternoon."

Then actually pass it on, in the same place every time, whether that's a shared chat, a notebook or an email.

## Handling upset callers

Some callers are frustrated before you say a word. Your aim isn't to win the argument. It's to calm things down enough to solve the problem.

- **Let them finish.** Interrupting makes it worse. Take notes while they talk.
- **Acknowledge the feeling.** "I understand, that's really frustrating, especially after you took the day off."
- **Don't take it personally.** They're upset about the situation, not you.
- **Say what you can do.** "Here's what I can do right now..." beats explaining what you can't.
- **Give a clear next step and time.** "I'll speak with the technician and call you back by 4pm today."
- **Follow through.** A kept promise turns many complaints around.

If a caller becomes abusive, it's fine to set a boundary: "I want to help, but I need us to keep this respectful. If not, I'll need to end the call and we can try again later."

## Ending the call well

The last few seconds are what callers remember. Summarize, confirm, and thank them.

- "So I've booked you for Thursday at 10am. You'll get a text the day before. Anything else I can help with?"
- "Dana will call you back before 3 today. Thanks for calling Harbor Electric, Lisa."

Let the caller hang up first when you can. It avoids cutting them off if they remember one more thing.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is the best way to answer a business phone?

Greet with your business name, your own name and an offer to help, for example "Harbor Electric, this is Sam, how can I help?" Keep it short and friendly.

### Should I say good morning or just hello?

Either works. "Good morning" or "Good afternoon" sounds a little warmer, but only if you'll remember to switch after noon. "Thanks for calling" is a safe all-day option.

### How long is it acceptable to keep someone on hold?

Aim for under a minute, and check in if it runs longer. For anything that will take more than a couple of minutes, offer to call back.

### What should I do if I can't answer the caller's question?

Be honest, say who can, and give a specific time they'll hear back. Then make sure the message reaches that person.

### How do I sound professional when answering on my cellphone?

Use a separate business number so you know which calls are business calls, and answer them with your business greeting. Step somewhere quiet before you pick up, if you can.
