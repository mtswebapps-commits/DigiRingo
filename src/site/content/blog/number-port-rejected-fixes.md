Few things are more frustrating than waiting several days for a number port, only to hear it was rejected. Often the reason is something tiny: a missing suite number, a business name written slightly differently, or a PIN that expired.

Rejections feel arbitrary, but they exist for a good reason. Carriers check port requests strictly to stop "slamming" and number hijacking, where someone steals a phone number by porting it away without permission. That strictness protects you, even when it slows you down.

The good news is that almost every rejection has a clear fix. This guide lists the most common reasons, explains what each one actually means, and tells you how to correct it and resubmit quickly.

> **Key takeaway:** Before you submit a port, copy the account name, service address, account number and transfer PIN directly from your current carrier's records. Most rejections come from details that don't match exactly.

## The most common rejection reasons

Here's a quick reference. The sections below explain each one in more detail.

| Rejection reason | What it means | How to fix it |
|---|---|---|
| Name mismatch | Account holder name doesn't match records | Use the exact name on the carrier account |
| Address mismatch | Service address doesn't match | Request the service address on file and copy it exactly |
| Invalid account number | Account number is wrong or incomplete | Get it from the bill or ask the carrier directly |
| Invalid or missing PIN | Transfer PIN is wrong, missing or expired | Generate a new port-out PIN |
| Port protection or freeze | The account blocks ports | Remove the lock with your carrier |
| Number not on account | The number belongs to a different account | Find the right account or account holder |
| Partial port issue | Moving some numbers would leave the account broken | Reassign the main billing number first, or port all numbers |
| Pending order or change | The old carrier has an open order on the account | Wait for it to finish or cancel it |
| Number not portable to provider | The gaining provider can't serve that area | Choose another provider or keep the number where it is |

## Mismatched name or address

This is the number one reason ports fail.

**Name issues** happen when the account is under a person but the port request uses the business name, or the other way round. They also happen with abbreviations ("Co." vs "Company"), punctuation, old business names after a rebrand, or a spouse or former partner being the actual account holder.

**Address issues** happen because carriers check the *service address*, which is where the line was installed or registered. It's not always your billing address. A business that moved offices may still have the old address on file. Missing unit or suite numbers, or different spellings of a street, also trigger rejections.

How to fix it:

- Ask your current carrier for a **Customer Service Record (CSR)**. This document shows exactly what they have on file.
- Copy the name and service address from the CSR character for character.
- If the account holder is someone else, they need to authorize the port, or you need to transfer the account into your name first.

## Wrong account number or PIN

The account number on a mobile bill isn't always the one carriers use for ports. Some carriers use a separate account number for ports, and many now require a dedicated **port-out PIN** or transfer PIN. These PINs are often generated in the carrier's app or by calling customer service, and some expire after a few days.

How to fix it:

- Confirm the correct account number with your carrier, specifically "the account number for porting."
- Generate a new transfer PIN right before submitting.
- For business accounts, check if a separate business account number or authorized-user PIN applies.
- Don't confuse your voicemail PIN or online login password with the port-out PIN. They're different.

## Number still under contract or locked

A carrier can't block a valid port because you're under contract. But a contract can still cause trouble in two ways.

First, some accounts have **port protection** or a **number lock** switched on. This is a security feature, often enabled by default or added after fraud attempts. While it's on, every port request is rejected automatically.

Second, leaving a contract early may trigger **early termination fees** or require you to pay off a device. These don't stop the port, but they'll appear on your final bill.

How to fix it:

- Ask your carrier to remove port protection or the number lock, then resubmit.
- Ask about any fees before you port, so there are no surprises.
- If you have unpaid balances, sort them out. They don't legally block a port, but they can make the old carrier less helpful.

## Partial ports and multi-line accounts

A **partial port** is when you move only some numbers off an account and leave others behind. This is common when a business moves its main lines but keeps a fax line or alarm line with the old carrier.

The tricky part is the **billing telephone number (BTN)**, the main number the account is organized around. If you try to port the BTN but leave other numbers behind, the old carrier has to restructure the account, and many will reject the request until you tell them which remaining number becomes the new BTN.

How to fix it:

1. List every number on the account and decide which ones are moving.
2. If the BTN is moving, ask your old carrier to assign a new BTN to the numbers staying behind first.
3. Alternatively, port all the numbers and cancel the ones you don't need afterward.
4. Check whether any lines have special services like hunt groups or DSL attached, and remove them if necessary.

| Situation | Usual approach |
|---|---|
| Moving every number on the account | Full port, simplest option |
| Moving some numbers, BTN stays | Partial port, usually straightforward |
| Moving the BTN, others stay | Ask old carrier to reassign BTN first |
| Lines tied to DSL or alarms | Remove or move those services before porting |

## Other reasons you might see

- **Pending order.** If you recently changed your plan or added a line, the old carrier may block ports until that order finishes.
- **Number not portable.** The gaining provider doesn't have coverage in that number's rate centre. Check portability before submitting.
- **Duplicate request.** Two providers tried to port the same number, or you resubmitted before the first request was cancelled.
- **Disconnected number.** If the old service was already cancelled, the number may no longer be on any account. Act fast and contact the old carrier, as it may still be recoverable for a short time.

## How to resubmit quickly

When a rejection comes back, the fastest path is:

1. **Read the exact rejection reason** your provider passes on. Don't guess.
2. **Get the CSR** from your old carrier if the issue involves names, addresses or numbers on the account.
3. **Correct only what's wrong** and keep everything else the same.
4. **Generate a fresh PIN** if the previous one could have expired.
5. **Resubmit the same day** if possible. Each day of delay pushes back the FOC date.
6. **Keep your old service active** throughout. Never cancel to "speed things up."

If a port is rejected more than twice, call your old carrier's porting or retention department directly and ask them what they need to see. Sometimes a five-minute call clears up a week of back and forth.

## Frequently asked questions

### What is a CSR and why do I need it?

A Customer Service Record is the carrier's own record of your account: name, service address and numbers. Matching it exactly is the surest way to avoid name and address rejections.

### Can a carrier reject my port because I owe money?

An unpaid balance alone generally isn't a valid reason to block a port. You'll still owe the money, and the account must be otherwise valid.

### How do I get a port-out PIN?

Most mobile carriers let you generate one in their app, on their website, or by calling support. Business accounts may have a different process.

### How long does a resubmission take?

Once resubmitted with correct details, the request usually goes back through validation within a few business days. Simple ports can be much faster.
