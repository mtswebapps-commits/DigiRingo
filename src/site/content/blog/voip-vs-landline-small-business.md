For decades, a business phone meant a landline: a physical line from the phone company, a desk phone, and a monthly bill that rarely changed. Today most new small businesses never install one. They use VoIP, which carries calls over the internet and lets the same number ring a laptop, a smartphone, or a desk phone.

That doesn't mean the landline is always wrong. It has real strengths, and some businesses have good reasons to keep one. The goal of this guide is to help you make the decision on facts rather than habit, by comparing cost, call quality, reliability, mobility and features.

We'll also run through rough monthly cost scenarios for a one-person business, a five-person team and a fifteen-person team, so you can see how the numbers change as you grow.

> **Key takeaway:** For most small businesses, VoIP costs less, works from anywhere, and includes features a landline charges extra for. A landline still makes sense for locations without dependable internet or for equipment such as alarm panels that need an analog line.

## Monthly and setup costs compared

Landline pricing varies a lot by region and carrier, so treat any figure as a rough guide. Business landlines are typically billed per line per month, with long-distance minutes, features like voicemail or call forwarding, and taxes and regulatory fees layered on top. Setup can include installation charges and the cost of desk phones. Many businesses also sign multi-year contracts.

VoIP pricing works differently. With a pay-as-you-go provider you pay a monthly fee for each phone number and a per-minute rate for calls. There's no line to install, and you can use devices you already own.

## Call quality and outage risk

Landlines have a well-earned reputation for consistent sound. The line is dedicated to your call, so there's nothing competing with it.

VoIP quality depends on your internet connection. On stable broadband, internet calls sound just as clear, and often clearer, because modern codecs can carry a wider range of sound than an old analog line. Problems like choppy audio or delay are almost always caused by weak Wi-Fi, a congested connection, or a misconfigured router. Those are fixable, and our guide to [fixing VoIP call quality](/blog/fix-voip-call-quality) covers them step by step.

Outages are the bigger difference:

- **Internet outage:** VoIP calls in the app stop working until the connection returns. A good setup forwards unanswered calls to a cellphone, so customers still reach you over the mobile network.
- **Power outage:** Traditional copper landlines often keep working because the phone company supplies power down the line. However, many "landlines" today are actually delivered over fiber or cable and need power at your premises too, so check what you really have.
- **Local disaster:** If your office is closed, a landline only rings in that empty building. VoIP rings wherever your team is.

## Mobility and remote work

This is where VoIP pulls clearly ahead. A landline number rings on phones physically plugged into that line. If you're at a client's site, at home, or traveling, you either miss the call or pay for forwarding.

With VoIP, your business number lives in an app. You can answer at your desk, on your phone in the car park, or on a laptop in another country, and outgoing calls show your business number either way. Hiring someone remote doesn't require a new line installation, just a new login or number.

For small businesses that work in the field, like trades, cleaners, consultants or real estate agents, that mobility is often the whole reason to switch.

## Features you get by default

Landline features are typically extras: voicemail, call waiting, forwarding and caller ID may each carry a monthly charge, and anything more advanced usually needs an on-site phone system.

VoIP services tend to include the basics and add software features over time. Common ones include:

- Answering calls in a browser or mobile app
- Ringing several devices at the same time
- Forwarding to a cellphone when you don't answer
- Voicemail
- Call history
- Business texting from the same number
- Numbers in other cities or countries

Not every VoIP provider offers every feature, so check the list against what you actually need rather than what sounds impressive.

## When keeping a landline still makes sense

There are honest reasons to keep one:

- **No dependable internet.** If your location has unreliable broadband and poor mobile coverage, a landline may be the only dependable option.
- **Analog equipment.** Some alarm systems, elevator phones, fax machines, and card terminals expect an analog line. Check with the equipment vendor before cutting over.
- **Specific regulatory or contractual requirements.** A few industries or buildings require a fixed line for safety reasons.
- **A long contract.** If you're locked in, it may be cheaper to run VoIP alongside the landline until the contract ends.

A common middle ground is to keep a single landline for the equipment that needs it and move your customer-facing number and team calls to VoIP. You can also forward the landline to a mobile phone in the meantime, a feature many landline carriers offer, sometimes for an extra fee.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is VoIP cheaper than a landline?

In most cases, yes. VoIP avoids line rental and installation, and per-minute rates are low. The savings usually grow with every extra line and with international calling.

### Is VoIP reliable enough for a business?

On a stable internet connection, yes. The main risk is an internet outage, which you can cover by forwarding unanswered calls to a cellphone.

### Will VoIP work with my existing desk phone?

Older analog desk phones generally won't plug directly into a VoIP service. Most small businesses use a computer, smartphone, or headset instead.

### Can I keep my landline number if I switch to VoIP?

Usually you can port a landline number to a new provider. DIGIRINGO doesn't offer porting yet, but you can forward your landline to your DIGIRINGO number in the meantime.

### Do I need faster internet for VoIP?

Probably not. Each call needs roughly 100 kbps in each direction, which nearly any broadband plan handles. Stability matters more than raw speed.
