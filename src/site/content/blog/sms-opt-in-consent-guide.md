Texting is one of the most effective ways to reach customers, which is exactly why it's regulated. In the US, sending the wrong kind of text to someone who didn't agree to it can lead to carrier blocks, complaints, and in the worst cases expensive lawsuits.

The rules sound intimidating, but the core idea is simple: get clear permission before you text, tell people what they're signing up for, make it easy to stop, and keep a record. Businesses that follow those four habits avoid nearly all of the trouble.

This guide covers the basics of US consent law, practical ways to collect opt-ins, example wording you can adapt, how to handle STOP and HELP, sensible sending hours, and what records to keep. It's general information, not legal advice, so talk to a lawyer about your specific situation, especially if you send marketing at scale.

> **Key takeaway:** Get permission before you text, explain what kind of messages and how often, include "Reply STOP to opt out," honor opt-outs right away, and keep a dated record of every consent. Marketing texts need the strongest consent of all.

## Why consent matters (TCPA basics)

The main US law is the **Telephone Consumer Protection Act (TCPA)**, passed in 1991 and applied to text messages by the FCC and the courts. It restricts calls and texts sent using automated systems and sets rules around consent.

A few points worth knowing:

- **Marketing texts generally need prior express written consent.** That means a clear, signed agreement (an online checkbox or a keyword reply can count as an electronic signature) that specifically covers marketing texts from your business.
- **Informational texts need consent too,** but the standard is generally lower. A customer who gives you their number for appointment reminders has typically consented to those reminders.
- **Consent can't be a condition of purchase** for marketing messages. You can't require someone to agree to promotional texts to buy from you.
- **Penalties are per message.** The TCPA allows statutory damages per violating text, commonly cited as $500 and up to $1,500 if the violation is willful. Multiplied across a list, that adds up quickly.
- **States add their own rules.** Several states have "mini-TCPA" laws with stricter consent, timing or frequency rules.

Separately, carriers and the wireless industry group CTIA publish messaging guidelines. These aren't laws, but carriers enforce them through filtering and registration requirements like [A2P 10DLC](/blog/what-is-a2p-10dlc).

| Message type | Example | Typical consent needed |
|---|---|---|
| Conversational | Replying to a customer who texted you first | The customer started the conversation |
| Informational | Appointment reminder, order update | Customer gave their number for that purpose |
| Marketing | Sale announcement, coupon, new product | Prior express written consent |

## Ways customers can opt in

There are many valid ways to collect consent. What matters is that the person takes a clear action and sees what they're agreeing to.

1. **Website forms.** An unchecked checkbox next to the phone field with consent wording beside it.
2. **Text-to-join keywords.** The customer texts a word like JOIN to your number and receives a confirmation.
3. **Checkout or booking.** A consent checkbox during online checkout or appointment booking.
4. **Paper forms.** Intake forms or sign-up sheets with consent wording and a signature.
5. **Verbal consent.** For informational messages, a customer saying yes on the phone, noted in your records. This is weaker evidence for marketing, so use written methods there.
6. **QR codes.** A QR code that opens a pre-filled text with your keyword.

What doesn't count: buying a list, scraping numbers from websites, assuming consent because someone emailed you, or adding everyone who ever called you to a marketing list.

## Example opt-in language

Clear wording does two jobs: it gets real consent and it satisfies carrier reviewers. Adapt these examples to your business.

### Website or booking form checkbox

"I agree to receive appointment reminders and service updates by text from Greenleaf Landscaping at the number provided. Msg frequency varies. Msg & data rates may apply. Reply STOP to opt out, HELP for help. Consent is not a condition of purchase."

### Marketing checkbox (separate from service messages)

"Yes, send me offers and news by text from Greenleaf Landscaping. Up to 4 msgs/month. Msg & data rates may apply. Reply STOP to cancel, HELP for help. Consent is not required to buy."

### Keyword confirmation reply

"Greenleaf Landscaping: you're subscribed to seasonal offers. Up to 4 msgs/month. Msg & data rates may apply. Reply HELP for help, STOP to cancel."

### Paper or in-store sign-up

"By writing my mobile number below, I agree to receive text messages from Bloom Floral Studio about orders and occasional offers. Msg & data rates may apply. Reply STOP to unsubscribe."

Keep marketing consent separate from service consent. Someone who wants reminders hasn't necessarily agreed to promotions.

## Handling STOP, HELP and opt-outs

Opt-outs must be easy and fast.

- **Support standard keywords.** STOP is universal. Many senders also treat END, CANCEL, UNSUBSCRIBE, QUIT and STOPALL as opt-outs.
- **Send one confirmation, then stop.** "You've been unsubscribed and won't receive more messages." Nothing promotional in that reply.
- **Respect any reasonable request.** If someone replies "please stop texting me," treat it as an opt-out even without the keyword. Current FCC rules require honoring revocation made by any reasonable means, within a short time limit; the safest practice is to stop immediately.
- **Answer HELP.** Reply with your business name and a way to get support, such as a phone number or website.
- **Update every list.** An opt-out on one system should stop texts from all of them.

| Keyword | What to do | Example reply |
|---|---|---|
| STOP (and similar) | Remove the number, confirm once | "Greenleaf Landscaping: you're unsubscribed. No more msgs will be sent." |
| HELP | Send support info | "Greenleaf Landscaping: call (512) 555-0134 for help. Reply STOP to opt out." |
| START / UNSTOP | Re-subscribe if they opted out before | "You're re-subscribed to Greenleaf Landscaping texts. Reply STOP to opt out." |

## Quiet hours and frequency

Timing rules protect people from being woken up or pestered.

- **Federal timing rule.** The TCPA's telemarketing rules restrict solicitations before 8 a.m. or after 9 p.m. in the recipient's local time.
- **Some states are stricter.** A few states limit marketing texts to narrower windows or restrict Sundays and holidays, so check the rules where your customers live.
- **Use the recipient's time zone,** not yours. A text sent at 8 p.m. from California lands at 11 p.m. in New York, and an 8 a.m. text from New York lands at 5 a.m. in California.
- **Stick to the frequency you promised.** If you said "up to 4 messages per month," don't send 10.
- **Space out promotions.** Even with consent, too many texts drive opt-outs and complaints, which hurt deliverability.

A good default: send business texts between 9 a.m. and 8 p.m. local time on weekdays, and be more cautious on weekends.

## Keeping records of consent

If anyone ever disputes a text, your records are your defense.

For every opt-in, keep:

- The phone number
- Date and time of consent
- How it was collected (form, keyword, paper, verbal)
- The exact wording the person saw or agreed to
- For web forms, the page URL and ideally an IP address or form submission ID
- Any opt-out, with date and time

Keep records for at least several years. The TCPA's general limitation period is four years, so many businesses keep consent records for at least that long, and longer if they still text the person.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do I need consent to reply to a customer who texted me first?

If a customer texts you with a question, replying to that conversation is generally fine. Don't treat it as permission to add them to marketing messages; ask separately for that.

### Is a checkbox enough for marketing consent?

An unchecked checkbox with clear wording, which the person actively ticks, is a common and widely accepted way to collect written consent online. Pre-checked boxes are a bad idea.

### Do I have to include "Reply STOP to opt out" in every message?

Include it in your first message and periodically after that, especially in marketing messages. Many businesses include it in every promotional text to be safe.

### Can I text customers whose numbers I got from an old invoice?

You can usually send messages related to their existing service if they gave you their number for that purpose. Don't send marketing without collecting marketing consent first.

### Does registering for 10DLC mean I'm TCPA compliant?

No. 10DLC registration is a carrier requirement for delivery. TCPA compliance depends on how you collect consent, what you send and how you handle opt-outs.
