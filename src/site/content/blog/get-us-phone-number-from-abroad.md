## Why a US number helps you win US clients

For freelancers, agencies, software founders and remote service businesses, a US number does a few concrete things:

- **Removes friction.** US clients can call you without international dialing or charges. Many won't call an international number at all.
- **Builds trust.** A local or toll-free US number looks like an established business with a US presence.
- **Improves answer rates.** People are much more likely to pick up a call from a familiar US number than from an unknown international one.
- **Keeps calls cheap for you.** Calling the US from a US virtual number over the internet usually costs far less than international calling from your local mobile carrier.
- **Separates business from personal.** Your US clients reach your business line, not your personal mobile.

It isn't a disguise, and it shouldn't be used as one. Be honest about your location and time zone when clients ask. The point is to make contact easy, not to pretend to be somewhere you aren't.

## Choosing a city / area code

US numbers come in two broad flavors:

- **Local numbers** with a city area code, such as 212 or 646 for New York, 415 for San Francisco, 312 for Chicago, or 512 for Austin.
- **Toll-free numbers** with prefixes like 800, 888, 877, 866, 855, 844 or 833, which are free for callers to dial and don't point to any one city.

How to choose:

1. **If most of your clients are in one city,** pick that city's area code. It feels familiar to them.
2. **If you serve clients nationwide,** consider a toll-free number for your website, or a local number in a major business hub.
3. **If you want to text clients,** use a local number. Business texting from local numbers runs on A2P 10DLC, which is the most common route.
4. **Think about time zones.** A New York number suggests Eastern Time availability. If you work US West Coast hours, a California number may set better expectations.

If you can't decide, start with one local number. You can add a toll-free number later without changing anything else.

## Calling and texting US customers

**Calling.** Once you have a US number, you call US clients from an app on your laptop or phone. The call travels over the internet to your provider, which connects it to the US phone network. Your client sees your US number as caller ID. Because the call is priced as a US call, it's billed at domestic rates, regardless of where you're sitting.

Incoming calls work the same way in reverse: a client dials your US number, and the call rings your app wherever you are. Time zones are the main practical challenge. Decide when you're available, put it on your website, and let voicemail cover the rest.

**Texting.** US carriers require business texts from local numbers to be registered under A2P 10DLC. That involves registering your business as a brand and describing your messaging use case as a campaign. Businesses outside the US can generally register using their home-country business details, though the options for individuals without a registered business can be more limited. Approval typically takes several days, and there are carrier fees. Unregistered business texts are likely to be filtered or blocked, so register before you start texting clients. Our [A2P 10DLC explainer](/blog/what-is-a2p-10dlc) explains it in plain terms.

## Receiving OTP/verification codes: what works and what doesn't

This is the most common surprise for people abroad, so it's worth being clear.

Many banks, payment platforms, social networks and online services send one-time passcodes (OTPs) by SMS. Some of them deliberately block virtual or internet-based numbers from receiving those codes, as a fraud-prevention measure. That decision is made by the company sending the code, not by your phone provider, and it can change without notice.

What this means in practice:

- **Don't use a virtual number as the security number** for your bank, payment processor, or important accounts. Use your personal mobile number or an authenticator app.
- **Don't buy a number expecting it to verify a specific service.** Some will work, some won't, and there's no reliable way to know in advance.
- **Short-code messages** (the five- and six-digit numbers many services send from) may not reach virtual numbers at all.
- **Use the number for what it's built for:** talking to and texting clients.

Being upfront about this saves frustration. A US virtual number is an excellent client-facing business line. It isn't a dependable tool for account verification.

## Costs and setup steps

A rough cost picture for a US virtual number:

| Cost | What to expect |
|---|---|
| Number | A monthly fee per number |
| Calls to the US | A per-minute rate, typically low |
| Calls to other countries | Varies by destination |
| Texts | A per-message rate, plus 10DLC registration fees if you text |
| Hardware | Usually none, just a headset |

Setup is usually quick:

1. Create an account with a virtual phone provider.
2. Add funds or a payment method.
3. Search for a number by area code and claim it.
4. Install the app or open the web app and make a test call.
5. Set up forwarding and voicemail so you don't miss calls outside your hours.
6. If you'll text, complete A2P 10DLC registration.
