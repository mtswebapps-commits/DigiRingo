Few things split opinion like a phone menu. Some callers like being sent straight to the right person. Others hit zero the moment they hear "please listen carefully, as our options have changed." Both reactions are fair, and which one your callers have depends mostly on how the menu is built.

An IVR (interactive voice response) system is the technology behind "press 1 for sales, press 2 for support." The caller hears a recorded prompt, presses a key or says a word, and the call is routed based on that choice. Big companies use deep menus with speech recognition. A small business usually needs something much simpler, and sometimes nothing at all.

This guide helps you decide whether a menu is worth it, shows how to design one that callers don't hate, and gives you scripts you can record as they are or adapt.

> **Key takeaway:** A small business phone menu should have three options or fewer, say the most common choice first, and always offer a way to reach a person or leave a message. If most of your calls go to the same place, skip the menu and route directly.

## Do you actually need a phone menu?

A menu adds a step between the caller and a human. That step is only worth it when it saves time on both ends. Ask yourself a few honest questions before recording anything.

- Do different types of callers really need different people? If one person answers everything, a menu just adds delay.
- Do you get enough calls that sorting them matters? A handful of calls a day rarely needs sorting.
- Do callers often ask for information you could give in a recording, like hours or an address?
- Are you trying to look bigger than you are? A menu can help with that, but a fast, friendly answer impresses more than "press 4 for accounts receivable."

| Situation | Menu helps? | Better approach |
|---|---|---|
| Solo owner, one line | Rarely | Answer directly, forward to cell when away |
| Separate sales and service staff | Yes | Two-option menu or two separate numbers |
| Lots of "what are your hours?" calls | Yes | One option that plays hours and location |
| Emergency vs routine calls (trades, property) | Yes | Option that routes urgent calls to on-call phone |
| Mostly returning customers who know you | Usually not | Direct ring, short voicemail greeting |

A good alternative to a menu is simply using two numbers: one printed on sales material and one on invoices or support pages. Each number can ring a different person, and callers never have to press anything. We cover this approach in our guide to [small business call routing](/blog/small-business-call-routing).

## Designing a menu with three options or fewer

Callers hold options in short-term memory while they listen. After three or four choices, they forget the first one. Keep it short and put the options in order of how often they're picked.

1. **Greeting (one sentence).** Business name and a short welcome. Skip slogans.
2. **Most common option first.** If 70% of callers want to book, say booking first.
3. **Second and third options.** Only if they route somewhere different.
4. **A way to reach a person or leave a message.** Many callers press 0 by habit, so make 0 do something useful.
5. **Repeat or fallback.** If the caller presses nothing, either repeat once or send the call to a default destination.

A few design rules make a big difference:

- Say the action before the number: "To book an appointment, press 1" works better than "Press 1 to book an appointment," because the caller hears what they want before they need to remember the key.
- Keep the full menu under about 20 seconds.
- Don't put marketing in the menu. Callers are trying to get somewhere.
- Use the same voice and tone as the rest of your greeting.

## Script examples by industry

These scripts are written to be read aloud at a natural pace. Swap in your business name and adjust the options.

### General small business

"Thanks for calling Brightline Studio. To speak with someone about a new project, press 1. For an existing project or billing, press 2. For our hours and address, press 3. To leave a message, press 0 at any time."

### Home services (plumbing, HVAC, electrical)

"Thanks for calling Ridgeway Heating and Cooling. If you have no heat, no cooling, or a leak right now, press 1 and we'll connect you to our on-call technician. To book a regular service visit, press 2. For billing questions, press 3."

### Medical, dental or wellness office

"Thank you for calling Parkside Dental. If this is a medical emergency, please hang up and call 911. To book, change or cancel an appointment, press 1. For billing and insurance, press 2. For anything else, press 0 to reach our front desk."

### Restaurant or café

"Hi, you've reached Corner Table. We're open Tuesday through Sunday, 11 a.m. to 10 p.m. To place a pickup order, press 1. For reservations or private events, press 2. For our address and parking, press 3."

### Professional services (law, accounting, consulting)

"Thank you for calling Hart and Patel Accounting. If you're a new client, press 1. If you're an existing client, press 2 and you'll be connected to your account team. To leave a message for a specific person, press 0."

### Property management

"Thanks for calling Oakview Property Management. If you have a maintenance emergency such as flooding, no heat or a security issue, press 1. For general maintenance requests, press 2. For leasing and available units, press 3."

## After-hours menu options

Your after-hours menu has a different job: set expectations and catch anything urgent. It usually needs fewer options than your daytime menu.

"Thanks for calling Ridgeway Heating and Cooling. Our office is closed right now. We're open Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. If you have an emergency, press 1 to reach our on-call technician. Otherwise, please leave a message after the tone and we'll call you back the next business day."

Things to include after hours:

- Your normal hours, said once and clearly.
- An emergency path, if your business offers one. If you don't, say so politely and suggest an alternative.
- A clear promise about when you'll call back.
- A voicemail option at the end, so nobody hangs up empty-handed.

## Mistakes that make callers hang up

Most menu complaints come from the same handful of mistakes.

| Mistake | Why it hurts | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Too many options | Callers forget choices and press 0 | Three options or fewer |
| Long intro before options | Callers wait 15 seconds to hear anything useful | One-sentence greeting |
| "Our options have changed" | Everyone has heard it, nobody believes it | Just play the options |
| No way to reach a human | Callers feel trapped | Make 0 reach a person or voicemail |
| Dead ends | An option plays info and hangs up | Return to menu or offer voicemail |
| Menu loops forever | Caller presses nothing, hears it again and again | Repeat once, then route to a default |
| Hold music for minutes | Callers give up | Offer a callback or voicemail instead |

The single biggest mistake is building a menu nobody needed. If you look at your calls and 90% go to the same person, the menu just slows down 90% of your callers.

## Testing your menu

Before you put a menu live, call it the way a customer would.

1. Call from a phone that isn't on your account, so you hear exactly what callers hear.
2. Press every option and confirm it goes where it should.
3. Press nothing and see what happens.
4. Press a key that isn't an option.
5. Call after hours and check the after-hours version.
6. Ask someone outside the business to call and tell you what was confusing.
7. Re-record anything that sounds rushed, muffled or too quiet.

Review the menu every few months. When your hours, staff or services change, the menu should change too.

## Frequently asked questions

### What does IVR stand for?

IVR stands for interactive voice response. It's the system that plays recorded options to callers and routes the call based on which key they press or what they say.

### How many options should a small business phone menu have?

Three or fewer is a good rule, plus a way to reach a person or leave a message. More options than that and callers start forgetting the first ones before the menu ends.

### Should I record the menu myself or use a professional voice?

Either works. Your own voice can feel warm and personal, as long as you record in a quiet room and speak slowly. A professional voice sounds polished but costs money and needs re-recording each time something changes.

### Is a phone menu better than having two phone numbers?

For many small businesses, two numbers are simpler. Callers dial the number for what they need and never press a key. A menu makes more sense when you can only publish one number, such as on signage or a directory listing.
